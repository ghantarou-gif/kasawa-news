US regulators eye kratom as overdose deaths mount

3 hours ago

The US government is paying more attention to kratom after the apparent overdose deaths of young people, but advocates say the synthetic drug hurting people is not the same as the supplement and users should be aware of the difference.

But chemical dependency specialists and law enforcement figures warn that the distinction may not be enough to curb an increasing number of fatalities and treatment center admissions for synthetic and semisynthetic kratom derivatives rather than natural kratom leaf.

Signs of a growing issue around kratom and kratom enhanced with the 7-OH alkaloid are beginning to emerge. Three weeks ago, the US telehealth provider Quick MD released a study showing that the substances now account for more than half of its new addiction treatment patients, a five-time increase from 10% in 2024 to 50%.

“In September, 55% of our new addiction treatment patients sought help for kratom or 7⁠-⁠OH dependence,” said Ryan Lissack, chief executive officer of QuickMD. The company is now advertising on TV.

The US Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) recently issued two notices recommending that 7-OH, or 7-hydroxymitragynine – one of the major psychoactive ingredients in kratom – should be placed under federal controls while legislation has been introduced to effect stronger controls.

The notice came less than a month after Ole Miss college students Aidan Hamilton, 18, and Robert Strang, 20, were found dead in separate incidents with packages of concentrated kratom-related products discovered at both scenes. Federal prosecutors have said the two students “died of conditions consistent to overdoses”, though toxicology results are still pending.

Promoted as a wellness product that’s often sold in corner stores and gas stations, kratom is a mild opioid derived from the leaves of Mitragyna speciosa (a tropical evergreen tree native to south-east Asia). It’s sold in various forms, including powders, teas, capsules, tablets, extracts, gummies, and raw or crushed leaves.

Enhanced kratom is the latest drug to register as a concern for health authorities and law enforcement.

“Any opioid, synthetic or semi-synthetic, has the potential to be addictive,” says Patrick Trainor, director of the narcotics investigation and drug control unit at the Pennsylvania attorney general’s office. “Any opioid that’s being sold as a wellness drug is of concern.”

A 2023 Tampa Bay Times investigation found that more than 580 people in Florida have died from kratom-related overdoses during the past decade. The paper notes that most of the deaths were caused “by a fatal mixture of kratom and at least one other substance, but 46 people overdosed solely on the herb”.

The National Poison Data System found that between 2015 and 2025 kratom exposure reports surged 1,200%. Estimates of how many people use kratom and 7-OH products in the US vary. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates there around 5 million “lifetime” users. The American Kratom Association puts the number at 11 million to 16 million , with retail sales estimated at $2.2bn in 2024.

Trainor, a former DEA agent who worked on drug interdiction in Philadelphia’s notorious Kensington area, notes a primary concern with kratom is that “it’s being sold from retail locations, there’s a lot of misbranding, or misrepresentation, to what the potency is. People might think they’re taking a legal dose but that may not be the case.”

While kratom is not approved for use in a medical environment, it’s often used to moderate opioid withdrawal symptoms. A development with this use is that the increasing strength of the kratom products containing 7-OH means it can create new chemical dependency issues while trying to solve existing ones.

“For people who are dealing with substance abuse disorder, kratom has potential to get people sick,” Trainor says, using a street term for drug withdrawal symptoms.

This is happening as some harm reduction medications – including the opioid dependency management drug Suboxone – are becoming harder to obtain because of strict distributor caps and dispensary policies.

A study by UC Berkeley found that despite the relaxation of prescribing guidelines, harm reduction drugs such as buprenorphine, one of the ingredients in Suboxone, is only carried by four in 10 US retail pharmacies, and that access is more limited in Black and Latino communities.

The Trump administration has voiced support and showed effort to limit the chemical compounds found in enhanced kratom.

“These are potent opioids being manufactured and sold in consumer products, often under labels that obscure their true risks,” the US attorney general, Todd Blanche, said in August.

The US Food and Drug Administration has said it has observed “a proliferation of products containing 7-OH, and has “serious concerns” about products containing the alkaloid, calling it an “emerging public health threat” . Last December, the agency said that the US Marshals Service seized 73,000 units of 7-OH products – valued at roughly $1m – from three firms in Missouri.

The American Kratom Association has called for a “critical distinction” to be made between natural leaf kratom and chemically enhanced opioid products and welcomed efforts by the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) and the Department of Justice to distinguish between them.

“The DEA and the Department of Justice deserve recognition for addressing the critical distinction between dangerous, chemically manipulated opioid products and natural kratom leaf,” said Mac Haddow, senior fellow on public policy for the association, in a statement last month .

The distinction, Haddow added, “is essential to protecting consumers without imposing an unjustified ban on lawful botanical products”.

The Manhattan addiction specialist Dr Andrew Tatarsky said the concerns around kratom maybe misplaced. “We demonize the drug because we don’t want to deal with all the other complexities over why people may be vulnerable to overuse,” he said.

Tatarsky said he and his colleagues in the field were not seeing “significant or rampant problems” with kratom. “But 7-OH is a potentially problematic substance that people should be cautioned and educated about.”

Kratom/7-OH dependency issues have begun showing up in rehabilitation centers. Dr Mohammad Sarhan, medical director of the Caron treatment center at its Florida campus, said the practice treats kratom and 7-OH as different substances.

“Kratom offers a mild opioid stimulant effect whereas 7-OH is a highly potent opioid. Patients using kratom we consider ‘other substance use disorder’, but people who say they’re using 7-OH we consider and treat it as a standard opioid use disorder.”

Kate Appleman, chief clinical officer at Caron’s Pennsylvania campus, said she equated the kratom and 7-OH distinction to the way cannabis had evolved from a drug with a small percentage of THC, its active psychoactive ingredient, to one that might contain up to 90%.

“Cannabis is something natural in nature, but when you start to change the components of it, it’s very different,” she said. “Kratom is very similar. People think it’s natural, it’s legal, but when you start to look at the way it gets manufactured, that’s the part that makes it even more problematic.”

From an addiction standpoint, Appleman pointed out, the brain doesn’t know the difference between fentanyl, heroin and OxyContin, and kratom because it affects the same receptors in the brain. Caron has started stand-alone relapse programs for people who have had recovery from heroin or alcohol but relapsed on kratom.

“We hear stories from patients that kratom is more potent than they had anticipated and they have the same symptoms of addiction as they do for heroin and alcohol. Their life is unmanageable, they have withdrawal symptoms and cravings – everything that is standard with having a substance use disorder.”

Sarhan noted that patients being admitted often express a sense of betrayal and embarrassment at becoming dependent on a product that is sold next to energy drinks. “They say, ‘How dangerous could it possibly be? The packaging said it was botanical, plant-based, all-natural – nothing on the label said it was an opioid.’”

Signs that a new era of opioid dependency is approaching are hard to miss. Los Angeles county officials have linked six fatal overdoses to 7-OH, while the San Francisco department of public health reported the city’s first-known adult fatal overdose involving a semisynthetic kratom-derived opioid in September.

The California department of public health issued warnings stating that 7-OH and kratom-derived products are illegal to manufacture or sell for consumption in the state. Eleven states have made it illegal, while Massachusetts and North Dakota have issued temporary bans.

According Sarhan, it’s a “maybe” on whether Kratom could be considered an epidemic, a characterization typically identified and declared by public health agencies. Since it was not scheduled as an illicit substance, Sarhan said, there were regulatory issues. “If it comes off the shelves, does it become an illicit substance? Are people going to switch to an illicit substance or seek treatment? I don’t know. But whatever it is, we’re still early on.”
