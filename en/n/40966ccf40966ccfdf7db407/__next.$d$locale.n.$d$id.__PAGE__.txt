39:T2766,Donald Trump’s unwillingness to heed warnings of suspected Chinese penetration of the tech giant Nvidia follows a pattern of the US president ignoring sensitive intelligence that contradicts his world view.

Trump disregarded repeated information that the company’s co-founder, Jensen Huang , was having an extramarital affair with an alleged Chinese spy that threatened to undermine Washington’s geostrategically vital goal of pipping China for global supremacy in the artificial intelligence race.

The woman, Alice Huang – no blood relation of Jensen – was also responsible for buying $4.8bn worth of Nvidia’s highly sensitive AI chips in her capacity as head of a Singapore-based technology company, Megaspeed. The chips were then diverted to China, despite US export restrictions forbidding such sales to a country Washington regards as a key strategic adversary.

The president was inactive even after Marco Rubio, the secretary of state and national security adviser – and one of the administration’s renowned “China hawks” – repackaged the intelligence into a classified report last May warning of the geopolitical risks posed by the relationship.

“It’s clear that the White House was warned, had adequate opportunities to take action against what was an emerging and then eventually very clear chip diversion network that was emerging under Megaspeed and other south-east Asian offshore data farms – and it just failed to act,” said Julia Curlee, a former Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) officer who specialized in the Middle East and China and also worked on the White House national security council during Trump’s first presidency.

“It’s the latest episode of the president ignoring or not believing the intelligence that’s being provided to him.”

Despite the intelligence concerns raised by his conduct, Huang was reinstated as Nvidia’s chief executive on Monday following pressure from the White House and the company’s shareholders.

Trump’s behaviour over the Nvidia affairs mirrors his conduct towards the war with Iran, Curlee argues, where he pressed ahead with military actions despite being forewarned of potential complications.

Trump’s decision to go to war with Iran contradicted advice that the country’s Islamic regime had the resilience to withstand attempts to topple it and was likely to retaliate by closing the strait of Hormuz – predictions that were subsequently borne out.

Trump’s dismissal of intelligence warnings in both cases recalls his comments at a 2018 summit with Vladimir Putin when he openly sided with the Russian president over US intelligence agencies, who had assessed that Russia had interfered in the 2016 presidential election.

His indifference to intelligence work and the professionals who perform it appears to surpass that of previous administrations, even those who were accused of deliberately politicizing the practice – an accusation leveled at George W Bush following the 2003 invasion of Iraq.

“There are other administrations that have ignored or or tried to politicize intelligence but I think the Trump administration stands alone in how little it seems to regard the intelligence analysis prepared to help the president make decisions,” said Curlee.

“The best example of this is the run-up to the war in Iran, where the administration just truly shut out the warnings which have been there and were provided for many years about what the Iranians would do in the case of a war.”

Trump’s administration has also fired agents for providing intelligence assessments at odds with the president’s own statements. Last year, Lt Gen Jeffrey Kruse, the head of the Defense Intelligence Agency, was dismissed over a report that contradicted Trump’s claim that Iran’s nuclear sites had been “obliterated” by US strikes carried out in June 2025.

“What makes them really different from, say, the Bush administration, is that even that administration did not publicly fire officers when they didn’t provide information the president wanted to hear,” said Curlee, who also served on the White House national security council under Trump and Joe Biden.

“I feel pretty strongly that the fault of the Iran war lies at this refusal to listen to the intelligence community.”

Curlee said the Chinese acquisition of the Nvidia chips compared in strategic significance to the case of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg , a US couple who were executed after being convicted of passing on atomic secrets to the Soviet Union in the early years of the cold war. That made official indifference to the intelligence discovery all the more remarkable, she argued.

“This is a technology at the heart of the strategic competition with our primary competitor in the world, and at an industrial scale, they managed to divert chips from our leading hardware company,” she said.

“It’s a terrible thing that’s occurred and we can’t undo that. But uncovering it and stopping it was done at least over a year ago. If the policymaker then refuses to do their job, there’s nothing the intelligence community can do about that.”

The administration’s stance has drawn further scrutiny by deciding not to provide a “defensive briefing” to Jensen Huang about the discovery.

“Before public accusations and scandals, it used to be accepted practice for someone to pull the American target aside for a man-to-man chat – to tell him to think with the head between his shoulders,” said Fulton Armstrong, a former CIA analyst specializing in Latin America who also has experience in Taiwan.

“The chat might even include discussion of feeding the foreigner some disinformation. It seems that four years of Trump’s first presidency, four years of do-nothingism with Biden, and now almost two years of Trump II have so deeply changed the intel community that practical solutions are no longer allowed.”

No explanation has been provided for the failure to warn Huang – thus allowing him to continue his relationship with Alice Huang. He was also allowed to maintain his already close access to Trump.

Curlee said it was a “policy decision [rather than] an intelligence decision.” She added “It is to me a very unusual suspect decision to let something like this go on for as long as it apparently did, without at minimum protecting this US citizen, who is the object of of a Chinese spy operation.”

Fevered speculation has swirled over whether Alice Huang may have been part of a “honey trap” operation set by China’s intelligence services to lure Jensen Huang into a close business relationship that would entice him into sending vast amounts of Nvidia’s coveted chips to a company which would them to pass them to China, subverting American law.

China’s sprawling ministry of state security (MSS) is believed to use the technique, which most western intelligence agencies publicly insist they do not use, on the grounds that is unethical and potentially counterproductive, because it often depends on blackmail.

But analysts doubt that it occurred in this instance. Alice Huang, they suggest, could have disclosed the relationship to Chinese agents after it began because she was legally obliged to do so under China’s catch-all intelligence law, which legally obliges the country’s citizens to “cooperate” with security services on matters defined as “national security”. That term has a much broader definition under China’s laws than in most western countries, and can cover commercial and economic development issues.

Frank Montoya, a retired FBI agent who specialized on China, said a straightforward “honey trap” was unlikely.

“I don’t think that it was it was mapped out specifically where the MSS officer said we’re going to find this person and we’re going to set her up for business and then target her against against the CEO of [Nvidia],” he said.

“There was a confluence of opportunities. She’s very ambitious, very smart and she has this idea, and doesn’t necessarily brings it to the MSS, but is talking to somebody else. She has connections in the party, and she’s talking to somebody else, and then somebody else talks to the MSS and says, ‘Hey, let’s see if we can take advantage of that’.”

He added: “This was a multibillion-dollar purchase [of Nvidia chips]. There’s no way the MSS alone can fund that. They’re going to need somebody higher up [in the Chinese Communist party] to not only approve that but to provide the money when the deal is made.”

The operation, Montoya said, bore the hallmarks of the Chinese espionage approach, which he described as “whole of government” and lacking the “clandestinity” of traditional spycraft.

Alice Huang would have drawn the attention of US intelligence agencies through Megaspeed’s mass purchase of Nvidia chips, and because it rented out services from its Singapore datacenters to Chinese AI companies, Curlee argues.

The intimate nature of her relationship with Jensen Huang and her links to Chinese intelligence are thought to have been discovered through surveillance of her phone, almost certainly carried out by the National Security Agency.
