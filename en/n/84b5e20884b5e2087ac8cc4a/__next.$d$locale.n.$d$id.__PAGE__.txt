23:T1653,A I is adding to the cognitive load of workers already stretched thin by unpredictable pay, insecure housing and mounting debt – and the lowest paid feel it most. We need to rethink welfare for an economy being reshaped by AI.

Throughout the history of the study of economics, the human brain, which directs our labour, judgment and resilience, has rarely been considered an asset in its own right. The emerging AI economy is changing that. As machines take on routine work, what becomes most valuable is the judgment, creativity and insights only people can provide. Economists coined a term for this in recent years: brain capital.

The World Economic Forum finds analytical thinking is now the skill employers value most, and estimates that 59 in every 100 workers will need training to adapt to this shift by 2030.

In September, I found myself at the first Global Brain Economy Summit in New York asking a hard question: how much room is there for creativity and learning when one’s mind is consumed by anxieties about paying rent or for food to last through the week?

There are two new indices that rank countries and US states on brain capital, using about 60 indicators ranging from child poverty to the prevalence of Parkinson’s disease. Yet they both possess a flaw: neither measures whether people’s livelihoods are secure – whether income is predictable, debts are manageable or homes are safe. The World Health Organization counts safety and security among the foundations of brain health, but nobody building these indices asked people what drains their mental capacity.

I call this missing liability “cognitive taxation”. It is a tax no government voted for, levied on attention, memory and the capacity to learn, and collected through rents, debts and welfare paperwork. Worry over insecurity runs in the background like an app draining a phone battery, and over years, the chronic stress wears down the brain itself. And AI is raising the rate.

Livelihood pressures stack up to exceed what one mind can handle. Poverty manufactures its own interest payments. Imagine a week in which a worker’s rent goes up and she loses a shift at the last minute, something common for workers without guaranteed hours. She puts the shortfall on a credit card. Preoccupied with finding the rent, she returns a benefits form late and loses part of her benefits payment as a penalty. Three small setbacks leave her household poorer in money, headspace and health.

Financial scarcity measurably impairs how well people think, but the effect lifts when the pressure does too, according to a 2024 analysis of 112,000 people in the Journal of Economic Psychology. Low-paid workers in rural India who received their wages a few days early were 7% more productive and made fewer mistakes. When South Africa lowered the pension age for men from 65 to 60, those who became eligible earlier showed slower memory decline and were about five percentage points less likely to develop dementia.

Though AI is increasing the value of human judgment, it also increases cognitive taxation. Automation makes work less predictable, shifting and shortening schedules, long before it eliminates jobs, as Walmart staff dealing with AI-set schedules have found. Meanwhile, overseeing AI tools also adds mental fatigue, according to research by the Boston Consulting Group. Workers required to retrain for the AI economy will struggle to learn new skills if their minds are already occupied by worries over pay, housing and debt.

Britain shows what insecurity already costs before AI adds to it. About 2.7 million people there are out of work through long-term sickness. And my institute’s research found that people facing insecurity across several areas of life carry an earnings penalty as large as the gender pay gap, one that roughly doubles after seven years. Those findings suggest that worry erodes how well people work. Now the UK’s AI minister says the government must seriously consider that the tech could have “ an unprecedented impact ” on the jobs market. Worries abound and compound.

When Elon Musk told reporters at the White House in September that AI would bring “universal high income”, he offered Silicon Valley’s favourite answer to job losses: cash. Yet a study funded by Sam Altman, giving low-income Americans $1,000 a month, found that mental health gains faded after the first year. Extra cash helps, but it cannot guarantee a place for your child at a nursery or a bus that gets you to work on time. These pressures already tax the minds of people in work, and no payment removes them.

The better answer to cognitive tax and job loss is universal basic services, providing childcare, housing, transport and digital access, free at the point of use. The principle is the one behind the UK’s National Health Service: people should be able to rely on essential support without facing a bill when they use it. Extending free childcare alone is expected to bring 60,000 non-working parents into jobs in the UK.

Every government should ask this question of every fiscal policy: how will this policy affect the cognitive load of the poorest people it touches? Britain, which takes over the G20 presidency after December’s summit in Miami, should put it to the world’s largest economies. This question belongs in every treasury’s fiscal plans, brain-capital indices and AI-powered public services before they launch.

Sixty years ago we learned to count the damage economies do to the air and water, and it has changed how we build infrastructure for the better.
