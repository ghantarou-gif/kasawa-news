39:T2934,A corporate plutocracy. A broken healthcare system. Massive and widening inequality. Cash-soaked elections. Angry people struggling to pay their bills and disgusted with lawmakers in Washington DC .

Nearly everything that pushed famed consumer-rights advocate Ralph Nader to launch the 2000 presidential run that made him a political pariah in Washington is front and center as the US gears up for the 2026 midterm elections. But this time, things are much, much worse.

Nader, now 92, has been seething from his home in north-west Washington as the Trump administration rips the guts out of the hard-fought consumer and safety protections he built in the 1960s and 1970s. He’s once again calling for a revolution in a Democratic party that still wants nothing to do with him.

“The best thing that could happen for the Democrats is for [Senator Chuck] Schumer and [Hakeem] Jeffries to be voted out in January,” Nader told the Guardian during one of several long phone calls, speaking of the Democratic leaders in both houses of Congress.

If Democrats win next week, they need to be bold to fix the country, Nader said: prosecute corruption, restore the federal agencies that have been destroyed, and really change things for the working class. He doubts Schumer and Jeffries will do it.

“You think they’re going to have that kind of energy and courage?” Nader said. “They have no stomach for that. They’re going to drag the Democratic party down. People want justice.”

As Democrats and their allies seek to defeat Trumpism and restore the US’s teetering democracy, it would be easy to dismiss Nader as a tragic footnote amid the current chaos: the man, although he and his allies vehemently disagree, whose run as a Green party presidential candidate cost Al Gore the presidency and left George W Bush in control.

Ralph Nader addresses supporters as his mother Rose Nader looks on after his arrival at the National Press Club in Washington DC on 7 November 2000. Photograph: Rick Bowmer/AP

But the ideas he’s preached for decades, rooted in the New Deal ideals that built the US into an economic powerhouse, are front and center in the midterms and beyond, after a failure to address the woes of working-class Americans opened the door to Trumpism.

For Nader, and many others, the central question is: does anyone in US politics have the fight to fix what’s wrong?

A series of progressive wins in the Democratic primaries and a flood of cash to Republicans has put everything on the table , from taxing billionaires to raising minimum wage to “Medicare for All” to campaign finance reform. Even the left’s most moderating voices and a few Republicans agree it’s time to rein in the power and influence of big business.

Democrats “believe in regulated capitalism, and that we have overdone the capitalism and underdone the regulation”, said Matt Bennett, whose Third Way thinktank pushes moderate ideas. “The fact that we have a trillionaire who is trying to control American politics is pretty good evidence of that,” he added, speaking of Elon Musk’s Republican donations – now over $442m for the midterm elections.

Tinkering around the edges won’t be enough, many believe.

“There’s a prairie fire rebellion taking place across America today,” said Barry Lynn, director of the Open Markets Institute, an anti-monopoly thinktank in Washington. “Tax policy is not enough,” he said. “People want revolutionary change.”

Ralph Nader, speaking as an auto safety critic, appears in 1966 before the Senate commerce subcommittee, which is investigating charges by Nader that he was harassed and intimidated by General Motors because of a book he had written critical of the automobile makers. Earlier, the GM president, James M Roche, apologized for his firm’s investigation of Nader. Photograph: Bettmann Archive

As a lawyer in his 30s working with political heavyweight Daniel Patrick Moynihan, then a labor department official, on auto safety, Nader was thrust into the national spotlight after he wrote Unsafe At Any Speed, which blamed car design instead of what the industry used to call “ the nut behind the wheel ” for the high rate of US auto deaths.

After he appeared before a congressional hearing in 1966 to testify about his findings, General Motors hired private investigators to, as Nader later said, “obtain lurid details” about his private life.

When the sting was reported, GM’s then president, James Roche, publicly apologized and Nader sued. He used the $425,000 invasion of privacy settlement as seed money to build a team of hundreds of lawyers, dubbed “Nader’s Raiders” who investigated everything from airline safety to air quality to the radiation being emitted from television sets, and left a trail of new laws and federal agencies in their wake.

Big business hasn’t faced as much pushback in the United States since, with the notable exception of the Consumer Finance Protection Bureau (CFPB) created by the Massachusetts senator Elizabeth Warren after Wall Street created the 2008 financial crisis.

Republicans, led by the Trump administration , have been trying to destroy the CFPB for a decade and appear to be close to succeeding . After Joe Biden’s administration stepped up enforcement of predatory practices, re-examined monopolies and championed labor rights, Trump’s White House has gone the opposite direction.

What Nader was able to do during the 1960s and 1970s isn’t going to work today. New rules for how businesses should act happened because of the energy in Congress then and Nader’s ability “to create an advocacy model and push through a lot of legislation and policy where business wasn’t ready for it and the times were demanding it”, explains Robert Weissman, the co-president of Public Citizen, one of groups Nader founded.

“The problem now is the times demand it and people want it but business is organized against it,” Weissman said. “It’s not that we’re not trying hard enough. There’s a different kind of pushback.”

Federal lobbying, or money businesses and other groups spend trying to influence policy set in Washington hit a record of $5.13bn in 2025 , a 250% increase from 1998. Trade groups representing industry, realtors and drug companies are top donors .

At the same time, “we’re dealing with an administration that’s both carrying water for corporations and wrecking regulatory agencies, against the backdrop of a hostile court system,” Weissman said.

The Vermont senator Bernie Sanders ran presidential campaigns on these issues in 2016 and Sanders and Warren in 2020. But no one has become an effective national champion for them in the way Nader did at his zenith. He was plastered on magazine covers and topped most-trusted polls, appeared on Saturday Night Live and Phil Donahue’s talkshow, and had the ear of presidents, Congress and major news organizations.

Bill Murray and Ralph Nader during the Weekend Update sketch on 15 March 1980. Photograph: NBC via Getty Images

“It’s almost infuriating that we don’t have somebody helming a consumer movement in this country given how central these issues are to the national conversation,” said Lindsay Owens, the president of Groundwork Collaborative, a Washington research and policy group pushing new laws to curb algorithmic pricing and other pro-consumer moves.

And no, Sanders, 85, doesn’t want to talk to him either. “Bernie hasn’t called me back in 25 years,” Nader said.

Nader once cornered Sanders in the Senate barbershop while the senator was getting a shave. “He couldn’t run away,” Nader recalled, so he grilled him about why the senator wasn’t meeting with him or other public advocates and organizers. “Look, he doesn’t want to be associated with me because of the way they tainted me on 2000,” he said.

Nader remains undeterred. He backed an impeachment seminar in July and predicted Trump could step down this month. Recently, he has been dialing progressive candidates including Michigan’s Abdul El-Sayed and Maine’s Troy Jackson directly, to present his policy plan he thinks would guarantee their midterm success even as money pours into Republican opponents. On the agenda: higher wages, universal health insurance, putting “corporate crooks” in jail and impeaching Trump. “The better their agenda, the less money they need” to win, he said.

Nader’s not optimistic about big changes if Democrats win both houses of Congress, however. “They’ll break open the champagne and go back to business as normal and start raising more money for 2028,” he predicts. “I can predict ’em like you predict the sun coming up every morning,” he added.

The 2000 presidential election that unexpectedly put Bush into power catapulted the country into a fresh round of corporate tax cuts and deregulation, as well as the disastrous Iraq war. Some liberals point to it as a turning point, marking the death of bipartisanship and politicalization of the supreme court.

Nader was first recruited to run for president in 1996 by a Green party dismayed by, among other things, Democratic president Bill Clinton’s welfare reform. The goal was to capture enough of the popular vote to cross the 5% threshold for a public funding grant in the next election, ushering in a new era of three-party politics in a country dominated by two for over 130 years.

Republicans and Democrats had cozied up to corporate power so completely that citizens and citizens groups like the ones he founded couldn’t do much any more, Nader said. “King Corporation rules in Washington DC,” he said in a May 2000 speech . “We have a government of the Exxon’s, by the General Motors, for the DuPonts.”

“He wanted to get his message out but he also wanted to help the Green party get 5% of the vote so they could build as an organization,” said two-time former campaign manager Theresa Amato, a lawyer and public advocate.

A precursor to Sanders’ presidential campaign, Nader drew thousands of young people in massive rallies. He was joined onstage by celebrities including Susan Sarandon, Patti Smith, Ani DiFranco and Eddie Vedder.
