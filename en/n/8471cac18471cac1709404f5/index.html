Fair-weather Silicon Valley pivots away from the right after blue wave: ‘I’m not Maga’

32 minutes ago

A short two years ago, Elon Musk stood before an audience of tens of thousands of people in New York City and proudly told them: “I’m not just Maga. I’m dark, gothic Maga.”

On Wednesday, Musk spoke with much less conviction. “I’m not Maga,” he said. “Well, at least I’m not sure what Maga means.” The richest person in the world added he felt “foolish” for spending some $500m to elect Republicans in the midterms. “If I could rewind the clock, I would have spent less money,” he said at the New York Times’ DealBook conference, adding that he did not have “some sort of blind adherence to a set of policies by the Republican party”.

Silicon Valley’s tycoons made a hard-right pivot during Donald Trump’s second term, courting the president both on X and in private meetings, donating millions to his inauguration and, later, to his pet project, the White House ballroom. Musk himself spent more than $200m through his political action committee, America Pac, to help elect Trump in 2024. The attempts to cozy up to the president appeared to work. Trump took office in January 2025 flanked by some of the very executives who once banned him from their platforms in 2021. Musk, for his part, was installed as the head of the so-called “department of government efficiency”, a newly conceived and temporary federal initiative.

After Democrats’ decisive triumph in the US midterm elections, though, many of the tech barons are retreating.

The CEO of Palantir, Alex Karp, was among the first to attempt a pivot, softening his previously hardline stance on immigration a week before Musk in an interview with Politico. The animated leader of the tech firm that has powered Trump’s deportation apparatus said that the president mishandled immigration enforcement.

“I was really happy to hear the president be honest that – you know, his words, I believe, were something like: ‘Yeah, we kinda went too far,’” Karp told Politico. “And I’m glad he’s saying in public now what I’ve been saying in private.”

Karp, who once described the company as “anti-woke”, broadened his retreat from immigration to right-leaning politics in general.

“I’ve been a Democrat my whole life. I grew up in a far-left environment. I know more about being a progressive than almost anybody on the planet, and I absolutely believe Palantir – all of our products are essentially progressive,” he said.

Karp’s latest comments stand in stark contrast with Palantir’s work with the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency (ICE), functioning, according to Lesley Nash, a staff attorney at immigration defense organization Just Futures Law, as the “corporate and data analytics backbone of ICE”.

“Look, we power ICE,” Karp said in an earnings call in November 2025. “We power efforts to defend America and Ukraine. And with allies. We’re on the frontline of all adversaries, including vis-a-vis China. And we support ICE, and we’ve supported Israel. I don’t know why this is all controversial, but many people find that controversial.”

“Our ability to win while sticking by what we believe is important for the world,” he added in the call.

Other Silicon Valley leaders had also changed their tones in the immediate aftermath of the midterms.

Alex Karp, the Palantir CEO, on the Claman Countdown at Fox Business Network studios in New York City on 27 July. Photograph: John Lamparski/Getty Images

Mark Zuckerberg, the CEO of Meta, is reportedly showing signs of shifting left. After the company bid farewell to Joel Kaplan, its president of global affairs and former adviser to George Bush, Meta recruited and promoted a more bipartisan slate of executives, according to Axios . Kaplan, who has been at Meta for 15 years, was promoted at the start of the Trump administration and was known for his ties to the the Republican party.

Many Silicon Valley CEOs have helmed their companies for years, and it’s not their first time maneuvering the changing tides of American politics .

Though a self-described moderate, Musk previously donated to the Democratic national committee as well as Barack Obama’s re-election campaign in 2012. In 2018, he celebrated Tesla achieving a perfect score on an index of LGBTQ+ equality in the workplace for a fourth year in a row.

“Don’t buy our car if that’s a problem. People should be free to live their lives where their heart takes them ❤️🧡💛💚💙💜🖤💖🌈,” he wrote .

Eight years later, having moved from California to Texas and donated hundreds of millions of dollars to Republican candidates, Musk struck the opposite tone. He fear-mongered about the Democratic candidate for Senate in Texas, by tweeting that James Talarico “will trans your kids”.

After a blue wave swept over the US, though, he seems to again be shifting his stance. Responding to a question Wednesday about Texas’s Democratic governor-elect, Musk said, “I don’t think she’s sort of a far-left governor, and based on everything I’ve heard about her, I think everything will be fine in Texas with Gina [Hinojosa] as governor.”

He described her as “quite moderate” and characterized his own politics the same way.

At Meta, Kaplan helped Zuckerberg ingratiate the firm with the Trump administration over the last two years, completing its transformation away from the once proudly left-leaning company it had claimed to be. Kaplan replaced Nick Clegg, who, as a former deputy prime minister in the UK, had taken a more global approach. When Meta was just called Facebook, the company had championed diversity, with a goal of ensuring half its workforce was from underrepresented backgrounds. Zuckerberg and other leaders started a group to help immigrants. The company also temporarily banned Trump’s Facebook account after the January 6 insurrection.

While Silicon Valley firms regularly sway with the political winds, few companies have done so while being as deeply embedded in controversial government policies as Palantir.

Palantir has a long history of working with the US Department of Homeland Security , first winning a contract with ICE’s investigative arm in 2011 under Barack Obama. In the ensuing decade and a half, it has cast itself as a friend to whichever party is in power while maintaining that business.

Under the first Trump administration, Palantir worked closely with ICE’s investigative unit, developing software that helped the agency investigate and arrest people using a searchable super-network of government and private databases.

As the first Trump administration came to a close, Palantir attempted to distance itself from the president’s aggressive immigration policies. In September 2020, just a few months before Joe Biden was elected to be president, the company called Trump’s zero-tolerance immigration policy that led to family separations “abhorrent” and said that it violated “basic human decency and human rights standards”.

Five years later, under the second Trump administration, the company was awarded a $30m contract to develop a software platform called Immigration OS for DHS that would “streamline” the identification and deportation of immigrants. The company’s products were “integral to ICE’s draconian immigration enforcement and deportation abuses”, said Nash, of Just Futures Law.

Juan Sebastián Pinto, a former content strategist at Palantir, now speaks out against the company’s defense and immigration enforcement work. He spent his time at the company crafting the language to market Palantir to government and commercial customers. He said the company and Karp have historically used public statements to appeal to whoever has power over government contracts. The company will have to work overtime to glaze over its involvement with the second Trump administration, said Pinto. The company’s tools have been used by ICE and in the Iran war and “really challenged our democracy, our fourth amendment, our perception of safety”, he said.

“Palantir’s priority right now is to shape the narrative as much as it can to avoid responsibility because it’s going to be facing a lot of questions from the incoming Democratic administration,” he said.

Palantir describes the tools it builds in a way many tech companies do: as neutral platforms that bend to the wills of the people that use them.

As Palantir decried Trump’s family separation policy in 2020, Palantir’s then director of privacy and civil liberties, Courtney Bowman, said that, though the company accepted the “moral implications” of working with ICE, its technology was politically agnostic and “serves the core institutional missions of our partners, not any political or ideological agenda or individual elected official”.

Among those customers is ICE, tasked with deporting all undocumented immigrants in the US. For Palantir, that meant working with and optimizing the work of a government entity that the company once expressed concern about, over the “potential serious human rights violations” the agency was committing.

Nash at Just Futures Law said that Karp’s purported concern over Trump’s immigration enforcement tactics fall flat.

“Palantir’s CEO may now express that he had doubts about the very immigration system his company enabled, but private expressions of concern will not mitigate or alleviate the harm that this system has and continues to cause,” Nash said. “Companies like Palantir must take a hard look at corporate accountability and complicity with the worst abuses of the Trump administration.”
