Trump crackdown shatters US hopes for Guatemalans displaced by climate crisis

1 時間前

T he small plots of farmland in Caparrosa, an Indigenous Maya Ch’orti’ village in eastern Guatemala , are bone dry. It’s mid-June, and the rainy season should have started a month and a half ago, but it’s barely rained at all. The maize is just a few inches tall, and some plots are completely bare.

Lidia Molina is nursing her two-and-a-half-year-old daughter, Karla, sitting on a dusty step next to skinny dogs and chickens. “We haven’t planted our maize yet; it’s too dry,” Molina, 30, said. “We’re hoping it will rain soon, or else we’ll have to make do with whatever my husband sends us.” She is thin and her daughter weighs only 19lb (8.5kg), more typical for a nine-month-old.

The Maya Ch’orti’ have survived centuries of forced displacement and ethnic cleansing by colonial settlers and feudal landowners, maintaining their traditional subsistence way of life despite losing land, water sources, ancestral knowledge and language.

But climate breakdown has pushed them to the brink. Guatemala is one of the top 20 countries most vulnerable to climate change, with two-thirds of the population expected to face water shortages by 2050, according to the United Nations Environmental Program (Unep). Indigenous peoples, who account for nearly half Guatemala’s population of 19 million, are often found in the most remote regions without basic infrastructure such as tap water or irrigation systems. It is these areas that are facing the most severe climate impacts.

In the past 15 years, as erratic rainfall causes subsistence crops to fail, thousands of Guatemalans, many of them Indigenous, are believed to have taken out loans or pawned small plots of ancestral land to fund migration to the United States in hope of finding work to save their families from starvation.

Donald Trump, in his second stint in the White House, has ramped up deportations of migrants, many of whom who had been living and paying taxes in the US for years, severing an economic lifeline for their families back home. Now, as rainfall grows even more unpredictable and dries up regional agricultural labor opportunities, rural communities in Guatemala are running out of options.

Caparrosa, where Molina lives, is located in the ancestral homeland of the Maya Ch’orti’ people, and is one of thousands of tiny rural communities in what is known as the Dry Corridor, a 1,000-mile stretch that covers almost a third of Central America, from southern Mexico to Panama. It’s a spectacular, mountainous and relatively dry area.

Lidia Molina, 30, with her child at home in Caparrosa in eastern Guatemala. Photograph: Daniele Volpe

For millennia, the dry corridor’s six-month rainy season began in early May, with a relatively dry spell midway through, known as the canicula . Millions of Indigenous and non-Indigenous subsistence farmers timed their plantings – maize, beans and other basic foods – to this seasonal rainfall. Few communities had water capture or irrigation systems.

By the 2000s, the changing climate began to have devastating effects, when heavy rain and flooding caused catastrophic damage in the area. These crisis conditions helped persuade the government to pass its 2013 climate change framework law, making Guatemala the second country in the Americas – after Mexico – to pass explicit climate legislation.

Then came the drought. The rainy season came later and later, and the canicula got longer, hotter and drier.

By 2019, five years of failed harvests had left 1.2 million people in the region needing emergency food aid. Children were dying from malnutrition.

Lidia Molina and her husband, Brian Pérez, are not their real names, which the Guardian is not disclosing, for their security. They were surviving at that time on just a few tortillas each a day, and several of their children showed signs of malnourishment, including distended bellies and hair loss.

The relentless drought triggered the first wave of mass climate displacement from the dry corridor: 188,000 Hondurans and 185,000 Guatemalans traveling in family groups were detained at the US southern border in 2019 , an eightfold increase from 2016.

By 2021, the drought had intensified and the situation in Caparrosa was desperate, and so was Pérez.

To escape the worsening drought, Pérez was about to embark on one of the most dangerous migrations in the world with his 10-year-old son Wilson, traveling more than 2,000 miles (3,300km) through Mexico to get to the US border.

To pay his “coyote”, a local guide for informal migration routes, Pérez had handed over the deed to the land where more than a dozen people, the entire extended family, live in a cluster of modest adobe shacks. The land would serve as collateral for a loan to cover the passage, which Pérez hoped to pay off once he found a job in the US.

Fast forward to June 2026. Pérez was not in Caparrosa. Wilson was, and he was 16 by now. The skinny teenager was taller and more solemn and he had a migration nightmare to recount.

The journey across Mexico was really tough, he said, but the US was worse.

Then US president Joe Biden had ostensibly stopped the family separations at the border that surged during Trump’s first term , but that was not the reality Wilson and many others encountered . “They didn’t let us cross, and separated me and my dad,” he told the Guardian.

Biden had also continued enforcing title 42, Trump’s pandemic-era executive order to automatically turn away most asylum seekers without hearing their cases.

Exhausted after a month traveling overland, Pérez and his son Wilson were taken away separately and detained by the US border authorities in a large multi-storey detention center in Texas. Their American dream was over before it had even begun.

“It was really hard [in detention], not much food or water,” Wilson said. “I didn’t see or speak to [my dad] for two months, until they sent us back to Guatemala.”

Wilson, 16, is Lidia Molina and Brian Pérez’s oldest son. Photograph: Daniele Volpe

Since then, his father has been spending long periods away from home, working south of the US border to keep the family from destitution – and to pay back the debt he owes the coyote. He has travelled as far as southern Mexico to work on banana plantations, and sometimes his family don’t know where he is or when he’ll be coming back.

Pérez’s absences mean more responsibility on Wilson to help his grandfather and uncles in the fields when he’s not in school. He held out a large plastic bowl full of maize kernels, saved from last season’s meager crop, ready to plant when it rains again. “It did rain a little a couple of weeks back, but we’d been too busy day laboring for other farmers, so our land wasn’t ready for planting,” said Wilson. “Anyway, we don’t have enough money for fertilizer yet.”

Ironically, synthetic fertilizer is mostly made from the same fossil fuels that, when burned, are the main driver of the greenhouse gas emissions causing global heating – and turbo-charging the drought and erratic rainfall in the Dry Corridor. The cost of synthetic fertilizers has also surged thanks to the illegal wars in Ukraine and Iran.

A week or two later, an update came through from Lidia Molina that Wilson had finally planted the maize after a lightning storm soaked the earth, more than 50 days late. But the rain didn’t last, and not long afterwards they lost the whole crop, Molina said, because the maize was too small and fragile then to endure the canicula .

Yet some communities in the region have found ways to adapt to the changing climate.

In another Ch’orti’ village, Corozal Arriba, just 15 miles north across the mountains from Caparrosa, farming families are producing an abundance of organically farmed fruits and vegetables: tomatoes, eggplant, peppers and potatoes.

Amid the worsening drought, and with no international aid or climate finance or government funding for long-term adaptation measures, the community worked with a grassroots Ch’orti’ organization called Comundich in recent years to build three relatively simple and cheap reservoirs and an irrigation system to pipe in water from a mountain spring. It was a process of trial and error, learning from YouTube videos and local agronomists, but the reservoirs have been a gamechanger: malnutrition and forced migration rates are way down, residents said.

Comundich has helped build almost 20 reservoirs across the territory so far, but there are hundreds of Ch’orti’ villages like Caparrosa where farmers are still 100% dependent on the rain.

Then in late June, just after Wilson planted the maize, which then failed, Pérez got in touch. He was in the United States.

Ten days earlier, he had crossed over the border near the Mexican city of Juárez and its west Texas sister city El Paso, on the other side of the international line that follows the Rio Grande. He had traveled overland for four weeks with 20 or so other Ch’orti’ subsistence farmers, all struggling to support their families. Pérez said that this time he’d used his father’s land as collateral to pay the same coyote, whom he now owes 145,000 quetzales, or almost $20,000.

“I really tried in Guatemala,” Perez said. “And I went to Mexico, working long, hard days in extreme heat in the banana plantations so the coyote wouldn’t take out the house. But you can’t get ahead, and I want more for my family. The weather has changed, it doesn’t rain when it should, and out there it’s just you, the crops and God.”

Nicolás, Brian Pérez’s father, rests at home after working in the fields. Photograph: Daniele Volpe

Droughts, floods, extreme heat, desertification and other climate-related disasters forcibly displaced 250 million people globally over the past decade, the equivalent of 70,000 displacements every day, according to the UN refugee agency. There is no reliable data on how many people from the Dry Corridor have been displaced – internally or across international borders – by climate change. But in the past decade and a half, thousands of poor rural Guatemalans on the frontlines of climate breakdown are believed to have traveled north in hopes of reaching the US.
