26:T149b,J ames Bond may be 73 years old but the superspy holds a licence to print money, at the centre of an evergreen multibillion-pound franchise spanning blockbuster films, merchandise, licensing deals and sponsorship.

The announcement that the Twilight and Batman actor Robert Pattinson is the next Bond comes seven years after Daniel Craig revealed that No Time To Die would be his last outing as 007.

This means that by the time Pattinson makes his first appearance it will have been the longest gap in instalments in the 64-year history of the film franchise, an agonisingly long hiatus for the centrepiece of a sprawling commercial enterprise.

Robert Pattinson has been announced as the next actor to play James Bond. Photograph: Scott A Garfitt/AP

While Ian Fleming initially struggled to get Hollywood interested in his debonair, womanising British novel creation, the big-screen debut of Sean Connery in 1962’s Dr No marked the start of a marketing dream.

The 25 feature films to date – the world’s second longest-running film franchise after Godzilla – have taken almost $8bn (£6bn) at the global box office, making it the fifth most successful property in cinema history, behind only the sprawling Marvel Cinematic Universe, Star Wars, Harry Potter and Spider-Man.

When Amazon acquired the famed Hollywood studio MGM – home to 4,000 film titles including the Rocky and Bond franchises, as well as 17,000 hours of TV programming – four years ago, some analysts estimated that the superspy could be worth half of the $8.5bn price tag of the whole library.

The estimate of the on-screen value of Bond was underscored last year when Amazon paid more than $1bn to take full “creative control” of the franchise from Barbara Broccoli and Michael G Wilson, the longtime stewards of the films who had retained significant power, unlocking the potential to develop a Star Wars or Marvel-like “universe” of film and TV spin-offs.

Plans for one potential spin-off in 2002 – a movie based on the character Jinx, played by Halle Berry in Die Another Day – were scrapped the following year, while the highly successful Young Bond series of books for young adults has never made it beyond print.

Beyond the box office, the carefully cultivated prestige image of Bond has proven a brand association dream, a marketing juggernaut impervious even to Daniel Craig’s critical assessment a decade ago that the spy was a “lonely, sexist misogynist” .

A Bollinger magazine advert linked with the 2015 James Bond film, Spectre. Photograph: Retro AdArchives/Alamy

Charlotte Mair, the founder of The Fitting Room, a communications and culture agency, says: “Bond is a brand in its own right, with associations of sophistication, adventure, luxury and craftsmanship, timelessness and, of course, Britishness.

“Bond is a standalone entertainment property, with its own identity, heritage, mythology and extraordinary brand equity. Its partners aren’t simply buying exposure around a film release. They are aligning themselves with a brand that has been building its own cultural currency and influence for more than 60 years.”

The 141-year-old Turnbull & Asser, the personal shirtmaker to the king – who bestowed the company with his first royal warrant as the Prince of Wales in 1980 – has created shirts for Bond actors since Connery appeared in the first film.

Lock & Co, the world’s oldest hatters, with roots dating to 1676, started its Bond relationship with one of its hats appearing in the now-famous opening credit gun barrel scene that debuted in Dr No.

Aston Martin has long been considered Bond’s sports car of choice, debuting in 1964’s Goldfinger, and a bespoke DB10 was created for 2015’s Spectre to celebrate a 50-year partnership with the franchise, but deals remain on a film-by-film basis.

The N.Peal cashmere turtleneck worn by Daniel Craig in the poster for Spectre sold out almost immediately. A grey, custom-made Tom Ford suit worn by the actor in Skyfall went to auction last month with hopes it would fetch as much as £40,000 .

The “Bond effect” lures big sponsorship deals, reportedly worth anywhere from $45m, with deep-pocketed corporates.

Heineken, which has partnered Bond films since 1997, has spent as much as $100m on sponsorship, product placement and global marketing supporting a single film – even if many fans were outraged to see 007’s “shaken not stirred” Martini staple replaced by a pint.

Omega’s long-running sponsorship, dating back to 1995 when it replaced Rolex as Bond’s timepiece of choice, included being directly namechecked by the superspy in Casino Royale.

Omega has a long-running sponsorship deal with the Bond franchise. Photograph: Retro AdArchives/Alamy

Various guesstimates have been put on the value of sponsorships, from as much as $5bn over the lifetime of the franchise to date, to about $200m a film in the modern era.

“If the Super Bowl is advertising’s biggest stage, James Bond is the ultimate brand partnership platform,” Mair says. “Some brands have become so intrinsically linked to the franchise that they are part of Bond’s DNA.
