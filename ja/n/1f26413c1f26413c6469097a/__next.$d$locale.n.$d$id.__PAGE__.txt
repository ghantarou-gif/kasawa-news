31:T2828,As millions of Americans begin holiday shopping this Black Friday, consumers and workers alike are already seeing how artificial intelligence is reshaping the retail landscape.

Anna, a cashier in a southern Oregon Walmart and leader with United for Respect, who requested to remain anonymous for fear of retaliation, criticized the implementation of AI at Walmart and its effects on workers.

According to Anna, AI is used for human resources tasks such as scheduling, assigning attendance disciplinary points, and through a safety alert system that she says is often inaccurate and often pulls her away from customer service interactions.

“During the course of a day on a given shift, I will get at least 10 or 15 safety alerts on my phone. I can’t move on through the app until the safety alert’s clear.” she said. “It’s very annoying when you get them. I need to go over here to help this young lady who’s crying her eyeballs out because she needs a pregnancy test, and I can’t get in to get her pregnancy test because I have to clear a stupid alert.”

She explained she has to take a photo and upload it after completing a safety alert, noting alerts can often mistake a shadow, a sticker, or something else as an actionable item, or will be flagged to an associate when it is a maintenance issue.

“If there is something that I clearly see that is on the floor, I’m going to pick it up. I’m not that ignorant to not to pick it up, but I don’t need an AI telling me to do it,” she added. “It’s not helping. This thing is supposed to be working with us and to help us and things like that. I don’t see how this is, because it’s not.”

In September 2026, several hundred Walmart workers had signed on to an open letter calling for the company to put workers first as it rolls out the use of AI in stores.

Walmart investors overwhelmingly voted down a proposal for a report on how the Walmart workplace is being affected by AI and automation in June 2026. A Walmart spokesperson cited an internal June 2026 survey where 84% of Walmart workers claimed they were confident using digital tools.

“Walmart’s approach to AI is, and always has been, people-led and tech-powered,” a Walmart spokesperson said in an email. “We use AI to serve our customers and members and better support associates – not to replace their judgment, experience, or expertise. Like any technology, these tools evolve over time, and feedback from the associates who use them helps us continually improve the experience and build practical tools that are useful on the floor.”

The use and implementation of AI in the retail industry is expected to continue to grow, according to industry leaders.

According to a survey of 56 AI leaders at US-based retailers published in December 2025 by the National Retail Federation, most retailers have or are implementing AI in a variety of uses, with plans to increase investments and implementation over the next few years.

About 75% of retailers surveyed reported implementing AI tools for coding and app development, 73% for office productivity tools, 66% for cybersecurity and fraud development, 59% reported preparing or implementing AI for supply chain and warehouse operations, and 45% reported doing so for marketing and advertising.

As AI use is becoming more commonplace, labor leaders and advocates have called for guardrails and regulations to protect workers and consumers, and protections in collective bargaining agreements to ensure workers have a say in AI use and implementation in their workplaces.

“Without proper guardrails, AI systems have supercharged surveillance for both consumers and for the workers who make it possible for consumers to buy what they need or what they want for their loved ones for the holiday season,” said Crystal Weise, policy & programs director at the AFL-CIO Tech Institute, a non-profit labor hub started by the AFL-CIO, the largest federation of unions in the US.

Weise noted surveillance of workers starts at the factories and warehouses where consumer goods are manufactured and distributed, to surveillance of consumers online and in stores, who have their shopping habits tracked and influenced by algorithms, and of workers, with these surveillance systems fueled by AI.

“We know that these surveillance technologies make jobs worse, and we know that it makes products and services more expensive. We need policies and collective bargaining agreements that stop exploitative surveillance,” she added. “We want to see humans in control of how shoppers and consumers are accessing the goods and services that they need.”

Weise said AI-fueled surveillance systems such as surveillance pricing , productivity quotas on workers, and monitoring dissent and workplace organizing efforts are already collecting reams of valuable data on workers and consumers, which is used to cut costs and drive up profit margins.

Shoppers may be subjected to AI before they even walk into a brick-and-mortar store, with several major retailers such as Macy’s, Target, Home Depot and Costco using automated license plate readers (ALPRs), and other retailers using AI tools to scale back Black Friday discounts and bolster their profit margins.

“We use this technology for limited, authorized purposes. Data is collected through ALPRs to prevent theft and fraud, help ensure security, and maintain the safety of individuals and our properties,” states Macy’s disclaimer notice on the use of the technology at some stores.

For workers, they may be subjected to AI from the beginning of applying for a job, such as at Amazon-owned Whole Foods, which uses an AI recruiting agent named Sage – the only way to apply for a job at the company, according to Whole Foods’ website on job applications.

Once in stores, customers and workers can be subjected to AI facial recognition technology, with major retailers such as Wegmans, Macy’s and Home Depot citing use of the technology, and customers are having their data collected by major retailers, such as Kroger, which archives data it collects on customers and sells it, according to a 2025 Consumer Reports investigation .

“We’re not anti-technology, but we are anti-dehumanization of the workplace,” said Stuart Appelbaum, president of the Retail, Wholesale, and Department Store Union and executive vice-president of the United Food and Commercial Workers.

Appelbaum argued AI can’t measure or be aware of life situations that should be accounted for in decisions related to discipline or job termination, and cited negative effects of algorithmic surveillance on workers.

“We’ve seen a rise in algorithmic surveillance, and it creates a pressure cooker environment. People are afraid to pause for a breath, or even to go to the bathroom, and for us, that’s really workplace harassment by algorithm,” he said. “Every worker deserves to have a human involved before any discipline takes place.”

He cited contracts the union has won at employers that include protections on how AI is used and implemented in workplaces, and emphasized the importance of being able to bargain over new technologies as employers are frequently using AI to try to lower labor costs and increase profit margins.

“A lot of retailers are using AI as an excuse to have fewer people on the shop floor,” Appelbaum added. “What you end up doing is diminishing the in-store experience, and I think that’s a huge mistake. Not only does it put pressure on the people who remain working, but it also, I think, isn’t good for the future of the store. Basically, we think the technology should be used to empower and strengthen retail workers, and I think that’s part and parcel of everything we negotiate.”

Fears of large-scale layoffs in the retail industry thanks to AI have yet to materialize. According to a July 2026 policy brief published by Stanford University researchers, AI’s impact on overall employment is “likely small” at the moment. But several firms have announced planned job cuts over the next few years, citing AI or AI investments as a leading factor, and new technologies, such as electronic shelf labels, are projected to result in job losses and increase prices for consumers.

In October 2026, Kroger announced plans to cut 3,000 corporate jobs over the next three years, citing the use of artificial intelligence to streamline operations.

“These notifications are part of the corporate headcount reduction the company announced in early October to lower prices and improve the shopping experience by simplifying operations and better use of technology,” said a Kroger spokesperson on the cuts.

The announcement came after the company was hit with a charge of $2.6bn in 2025 following its decision to shut down three automated robotic delivery centers, which Kroger did not comment on.

“As retailers feel the pressure from Wall Street to cut costs and pump up share prices, they’re turning to AI to do the dirty work,” said Milton Jones, international president of the United Food and Commercial Workers, which represents workers at around two-thirds of Kroger stores in the US. “Whether it’s AI-powered scheduling that slashes retail workers’ hours or surveillance pricing that raises costs for consumers, people are getting squeezed on both sides of the cash register.”

Bryan Wynn, national bargaining director for the United Food and Commercial Workers, said that investors are pushing for artificial intelligence investments at firms to cut labor costs, increase efficiencies, boost profits, largely without concerns for how these technologies affected workers and consumers.

He explained planning for these technologies has exceedingly become a concern in bargaining contract renewals and future union contracts, as retail employers are using AI for data collection and surveillance to influence and monitor the behaviors of workers and consumers, and to use that data to further increase profits.
