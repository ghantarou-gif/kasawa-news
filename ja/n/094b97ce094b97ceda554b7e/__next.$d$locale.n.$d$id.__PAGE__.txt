32:T216d,The European Commission has been accused of sugarcoating figures to enable it to release funds to Egypt under a landmark pact with the authoritarian government of Abdel Fatah al-Sisi.

The charge relates to a €7.4bn deal promised by Ursula von der Leyen , the European Commission president, to al-Sisi on a high-profile visit to Cairo in 2024. The three-year “strategic and comprehensive partnership” was intended to bolster Egypt’s shaky economy, deter migrants from coming to Europe and shore up a region reeling from the 7 October attacks and Israel’s war on Gaza.

The disclosure raises broader questions about the EU-Egypt relationship, as Egyptian authorities face scrutiny over shrinking press freedom following the jailing and alleged torture of six journalists , the entire newsroom of Matsadaash, a factchecking and investigative platform, and the closure last month of one of the country’s last independent news outlets and conviction of its high-profile editor , Lina Attalah.

Attalah is appealing against that conviction and will be in court this week as she seeks to overturn other charges including “publishing false news” , for which she was sentenced to a year in prison in absentia without her knowledge.

The spotlight is on €5bn in EU “soft” loans to Egypt – lending with easy repayment terms, known as macro-financial assistance, that is meant to be available only to countries that respect human rights and democracy. While the EU conditions on democracy and rights are vague, the loans impose much more specific requirements on Egypt to carry out economic reforms.

Separate data sources – from leaked EU documents and deleted from an Egyptian government website – raise questions about the European Commission’s claims that Egypt met targets linked to social welfare programmes, which enabled the EU to release €2.5bn in assistance.

In confidential documents, the commission told EU lawmakers that Egypt had “broadly fulfilled” and then “fulfilled” two separate targets to increase the numbers of poor people on social welfare programmes between 2024 and 2026. But data points to a net reduction of 85,734 households as the Egyptian government removed people from its welfare programmes rather than expanded coverage.

If Egypt had expanded the programmes in line with the EU’s apparent conditions – and not removed any households – about 245,000 households, equivalent to roughly 909,000 people, would be getting these benefits. (The Guardian used World Bank data to make this rough calculation).

According to the leaked documents, the commission wanted Egypt to “further expand cash transfer programmes”, but despite a net reduction in beneficiaries it gave the green light anyway to two separate “soft” loans totalling €2.5bn.

In response to the Guardian’s findings, one of the European parliament’s leading spokespeople on EU-Egypt relations, the Dutch MEP Tineke Strik, said she was introducing a question to the European Commission demanding clarification.

“The finding by this investigation that the commission is seemingly sugarcoating Egypt’s performance is worrying,” Strik said. “From the beginning, the commission has used macro-financial assistance primarily as a tool to lure Egypt into migration cooperation, while considering formal conditions and its reporting obligations towards parliament as an inconvenient formality.”

A street scene in Cairo. Photograph: Anadolu/Getty Images

Under the plan, Egypt has so far received €3.5bn in soft loans in three instalments, with a final payment of €1.5bn expected before the end of 2027.

The charge of sugarcoating figures relates to two social welfare programmes: the Takaful and Karama cash transfer programmes for poor and vulnerable households.

The Takaful (Solidarity) programme gives poor families cash payments as long as recipients have regular health visits and good attendance at school. The Karama (Dignity) programme gives unconditional cash payments to people living with a disability, older people, orphans and widows.

One of the EU conditions for Egypt to gain the first €1bn loan was to increase the number of Takaful and Karama beneficiary households by 100,000 to 4.7 million.

In a leaked report to lawmakers in the EU council of ministers and the European parliament, the commission described that requirement as “broadly fulfilled”.

The data in that report, however, reveals a net fall of 62,281 households during this period, owing to what the EU officials describe as “a multi-annual recertification process”. Some beneficiaries were added to the list but a larger number were removed because they were deemed ineligible.

In order to achieve a later instalment of €1.5bn, Egypt was required to “further expand” the programmes, adding 60,000 households to the Takaful and Karama beneficiaries list. A second leaked report to EU lawmakers shows that the commission concluded the target was “fulfilled” because 442,118 families were added to the list.

On this occasion, the commission did not report on numbers removed. Figures from Egypt’s ministry of social solidarity, however, show a net reduction of 23,453 beneficiaries to the programmes in the 11 months until June 2026, the period leading up to the release of that payment. The EU announced the release of the €1.5bn payment in July 2026.

The data has since been deleted from the Egyptian ministry website but the Guardian has seen screenshots and archived webpages.

During a period from 2024 to 26 when the EU required Egypt to add 160,000 households to the rolls of the Karama and Takaful programmes, there was a net decline of 85,734 households.

A customer pays for tomatoes with Egyptian pound banknotes at a market in Cairo. Photograph: Bloomberg/Getty Images

Timothy Kaldas, the deputy director of the Tahrir Institute for Middle East Policy, said the commission had generally “done a poor job of leveraging the funds of European taxpayers to support genuine stabilisation” on the other side of the Mediterranean. “The strategic interest of Europe is seeing a more economically stable Egypt with a population that is less precarious,” he said.

Sandra Gómez López, a Spanish socialist MEP who recently visited Cairo to assess human rights in Egypt, said the data presented by the Guardian raised important questions. “The commission must explain transparently why it considers these commitments to have been fulfilled and demonstrate that European funding is genuinely contributing to improving social protection for the Egyptian population,” she said.

She stressed that the EU should not withdraw support, “but neither can we allow the country’s strategic importance to lead us to lower our standards when it comes to human rights”.

For critics, the questions about the EU’s willingness to hold Egypt accountable on very precise socioeconomic targets do not bode well for the broader but less defined goals for democracy and human rights.

Strik said: “The fact that Egypt and the commission even seem to ignore the crystal-clear socioeconomic conditions doesn’t increase my expectation that they adhere to the vague precondition on democracy and the rule of law.”

Claudio Francavilla, an associate EU director at Human Rights Watch, said: “Without benchmarks, the EU has turned a human rights conditionality into a blank cheque for Egypt’s authoritarianism.”

In a statement to the Guardian, the European Commission said the policy target on Takaful and Karama referred to new beneficiary families “and not to its net increase”. The spokesperson said there was “no contradiction” between this statement and that in a confidential document that said Egypt was required to “further expand cash transfer programmes”.

Asked about the release of the remaining €1.5bn in promised loans, the commission said it “will be made once all conditions have been met”, referring to 24 economic policy conditions, keeping an IMF programme on track and “taking concrete and credible steps towards respecting effective democratic mechanisms, including a multiparty parliamentary system, and the rule of law, and guaranteeing respect for human rights”.

It added: “Compliance with the conditionality is examined thoroughly” by EU officials and diplomats based on “information provided by the Egyptian authorities and other relevant sources”.
