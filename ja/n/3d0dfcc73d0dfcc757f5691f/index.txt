32:T1fa0,T o call it a blue wave, said the Florida governor, Ron DeSantis , would be an epic understatement. The midterm elections were “the political equivalent of a meteor strike”. But one state, DeSantis added with pride , was able to “hold the line”. And that state was Florida .

On a night when almost everything else went right for Democrats, David Jolly fell short by 1.3 percentage points against Republican Byron Donalds, who will become Florida’s first Black governor, while Angie Nixon, bidding to become the state’s first Black senator, lost by 2.9 percentage points to Republican Ashley Moody.

Even the losses, however, were remarkable performances in a state that has shifted right in recent years and become the adopted home of Donald Trump , and where two years ago he defeated Kamala Harris by 13 percentage points . In an election where Democrats were winning in Kansas, Mississippi, South Carolina and Texas, Nixon was left asking: did Florida Democrats lose because the party didn’t support them?

“It was a missed opportunity,” the 42-year-old said pointedly. “I’m going to work to bring everybody together so that we don’t miss opportunities like that anymore.”

Democratic party leaders and major national donors made calculated investments in key battlegrounds elsewhere, producing stunning victories in unexpected places. Yet that generosity came at the expense of southern states like Florida and Louisiana , where local candidates were largely left to fend for themselves.

Was that because too many people had written Florida off as a red state? “Folks often write the south off. Now we’ve shown that Florida is in play, the south is in play, and I’m hoping that through my advocacy and me being so vocal that people will take note and will listen and donate to more candidates in the south,” Nixon said.

With nearly 24 million people, Florida is the third most populous state in the US. Covering its vast geographical expanse requires a huge war chest and saturation TV strategy. Nixon had neither. “I would say we were out-fundraised and Florida’s a very big state,” was her simple diagnosis.

Moody had raised $14.5m compared to Nixon’s $11.3m , according to the Federal Election Commission, while outside groups spent $10.4m opposing Nixon versus $3.6m spent opposing Moody.

Without the funds to buy airtime across Florida’s multiple expensive media markets – from Miami and Orlando to Tampa and Jacksonville – Nixon found herself overwhelmed by negative advertising from Moody and her allied political committees. “They hit me hard on it, and we didn’t have enough money to push back until it was too late.”

While conservative groups sought to depict Nixon as a far left extremist (“Angie Nixon is actually a socialist,” said one ), she believes it was a more traditional attack that hurt her the most. “Those didn’t necessarily work. What we saw worked was when they were trying to say that I was raising taxes on people.”

By the time Nixon’s campaign could afford to explain her economic platform, the ads had done their damage. Nixon believes that “of course” national Democrats should have intervened sooner. But she is careful not to dwell too long on the blame game.

“I wish more people gave me money, period. I’m going to work to move forward and work to organise and create an environment where, despite whatever the conditions of that year are, that we’ll still be able to win.”

Nixon’s optimism is bolstered by what transpired on election night in other southern contests, where underfunded candidates demonstrated that progressive, community centred campaigns can break through longstanding conservative strongholds.

She points to Scott Colom , the progressive prosecutor who became the first Black senator elected in Mississippi since the Reconstruction era. The lesson of those races, for Nixon, is that no red state is a monolith and no southern state is permanently out of reach if the party is willing to contest it.

Even with more money, however, any Democrat running in Florida faces a structural barrier erected over successive legislative sessions: the deliberate erosion of voting rights. Nixon points the finger at DeSantis. “That man has done so much as it relates to voter suppression . He’s done so much to suppress voters. Every single year they have attacked voting rights and the election laws and so now we’re dealing with that.”

While turnout rates among registered Democrats remained high, the overall playing field has been warped by the fact Florida now has 1.5 million more registered Republicans than Democrats, a huge reversal after Democrats held the advantage for decades.

Nixon argues that this gap did not emerge organically but was engineered by targeted legislation designed to debilitate voter registration drives. In recent years, Florida’s Republican supermajorities have enacted onerous penalties and strict restrictions on grassroots civic organisations.

She continued: “That gap is there because they made it harder for third-party organisations to be able to register voters. Historically, those third-party groups registered more people of colour. That was intentional to make it harder.”

In a state where vote-by-mail (VBM) had long been a secure and convenient staple used by voters of all political stripes, Republicans also changed the rules to require voters to renew their requests for mail ballots after every general election cycle.

Nixon added: “It was also intentional to change up the vote-by-mail laws because again, low-income working families and people of colour vote by mail more often because they’re working.

“They made it hard for that to happen and so we saw a huge drop. Thousands upon thousands of folks who would normally get VBMs did not get them because they didn’t know about the new law.”

In short, does Nixon believe she would have won on a level playing field? “Oh, absolutely, yes,” she replied without hesitation. “I do.”

It follows that Nixon refuses to accept that Florida, once a quintessential swing state but where Democrats have not won a Senate race since 2012, is now a lost cause. “We need to work to close the voter registration gap. We need to continue to organise and go meet people where they are.”

Nixon joined the Democratic Socialists of America over the summer, though she acknowledged some disagreements with the organisation. During her campaign she shared stages with the progressive stars senator Bernie Sanders and congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez .

But Nixon continues to resist easy categorisation. “I’m not interested in labels,” she said. “I’m interested in working with whoever wants to work to advance the agenda for working families.”

That agenda, she believes, is where Democrats can rebuild a working-class coalition that cuts across traditional partisan lines. When asked what the incoming Democratic majorities in Congress should prioritise in Washington, Nixon reels off an ambitious populist legislative platform centred on basic economic security.

“They need to pass comprehensive healthcare: push for Medicare for All . They need to increase the minimum wage. They need to push for comprehensive and compassionate immigration reform. They also need to hold Donald Trump and his people accountable.”

Does the Democratic party possess a coherent, compelling vision for the future? “We need more diverse voices in the conversation for us to get there,” Nixon said candidly. “I don’t think we’re there yet.”

Whether Nixon herself will launch another bid for elected office remains an open question. For now, her focus is on launching a political action committee, Never Scared , so that whoever steps onto the ballot in the future will not face the same structural headwinds she encountered.
