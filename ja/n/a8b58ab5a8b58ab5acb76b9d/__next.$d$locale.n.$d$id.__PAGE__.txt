1e:Td13,Judges have been told they “should always” inform juries to disregard rape myths in trials, as the official guidance was strengthened after criticism of the defence in Russell Brand’s trial.

The handbook for judges, which applies to England and Wales, was updated on Tuesday, and told them that a warning to jurors was “important and necessary”.

The Crown Court Compendium, which provides judges with guidance on directing juries in trials, previously told judges they “may give appropriate directions to counter the risk of stereotypes and assumptions about sexual behaviour and reactions to non-consensual sexual conduct”.

After the update this week, the guidance will say: “Even where an advocate does not overtly seek to advance the type of misconception it was once commonplace to encounter, directions designed to alert a jury to the potential error of adopting that kind of thinking remain important and necessary and should always be given.”

Judges can tell juries in sexual offence cases that people “react differently to the trauma of a serious sexual assault”.

Brand’s barrister, Ian Winter KC, was criticised for telling jurors that none of the complainants had “resisted or screamed or even tried to leave”. This, it was claimed, might have perpetuated preconceptions that could have spread doubt over the credibility of victims.

In the aftermath of the Brand case, Alex Norris, the justice secretary, warned barristers they could be barred from practice if they used rape myths to defend clients.

Norris said it was “unacceptable” for the profession to “perpetuate myths” about rape victims in courts, saying those who broke the rules could face disciplinary action.

Guidance in the compendium tells jurors that victims can differ in how quickly they report crimes, and that this does not detract from their reliability. It says some “feel shame and shock and do not complain for some time” and that a late complaint does not automatically mean it is false.

The guidance also states that providing assistance to juries on this topic has led to a “marked reduction” in advocates “seeking to weave myths and misconceptions into their cross-examination and/or closing remarks”.

It adds that, in some cases, guidance on rape myths may be appropriate to benefit a defendant.

On Friday, a leading prosecutor said the Brand case had shown that victims dealt with their ordeal in “wholly different ways”.

Siobhan Blake, the national lead for rape and serious sexual offences at the Crown Prosecution Service, added: “Since the verdict, social media has been awash with questions about how Brand could be tried and convicted for offences which happened almost two decades ago.

“Some have even cast aspersions on the women who came forward, questioning why they took so long to report what happened. But asking why someone didn’t immediately report sexual abuse misunderstands the reality of what it can feel like to experience it.

“There is no right way to respond to sexual abuse.”
