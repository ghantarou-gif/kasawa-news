5:T526,German reunification may be 36 years in the past, but when it comes to wealth, the divide between East and West Germany is far from history. East Germans still earn less on average, receive smaller pensions and own less property, according to Elisabeth Kaiser, the Federal Government Commissioner for Eastern Germany.

Kaiser, born in the state of Thuringia in the socialist German Democratic Republic (GDR) in 1987, has put the enduring wealth gap at the center of the debate. A study she presented in Berlin points to decades in which private entrepreneurship and opportunities to invest in one's financial future were restricted in the East. That meant, Kaiser said, that "families have not been able to acquire or pass on as much wealth." The consequences, she argues, have had a profound impact upward mobility.

How important are personal connections and social background?

But it is not only East Germans who see fewer chances of upward mobility these days. Kaiser pointed to the latest " Deutschland-Monitor ," an annual survey she commissioned to make her point. Although this year's survey is still being analyzed, early findings reveal that about two-thirds of respondents across the country believe that financial and social mobility depend crucially on wealth, personal connections and social background.d:X
