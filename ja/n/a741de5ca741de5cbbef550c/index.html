The Trump administration wants to give married parents money to stay at home. Will it be enough?

10月31日 10:00

Erica Hall and her husband knew that raising kids wouldn’t be cheap.

They intentionally had their children five years apart because paying for two in daycare would have been too expensive. When her first child was born, Hall had to self-fund her maternity leave by drawing on savings. And recently, Hall was laid off from her tech job, leaving her at a crossroads.

“Do we move to a lower-cost daycare? What kind of job should I look for? Can I dial back now?” said Hall, who lives in Seattle. And perhaps the most essential of all questions: “Do we need to completely 100% replace my income?”

More American families than ever consist of two working parents: 52% are dual-income households, compared to 31% in 1975, according to the Pew Research Center. Eight out of 10 of these families say the arrangement has been beneficial to their finances.

Despite this shift in family dynamics, the Trump administration has remained focused on an agenda that emphasizes a “traditional” family structure, where one parent stays home to take care of children.

As part of this agenda, the White House is considering a program that would pay parents $9,000 annually to stay at home, using reallocated funds that are now used for federal programs that typically support single mothers and low-income families, according to the New York Times.

The plan was reportedly proffered by JD Vance, a potential 2028 presidential candidate, who has proclaimed himself a defender of “pro-family” policies. The vice-president penned a Wall Street Journal op-ed in 2021 criticizing federal spending on daycare, claiming that “young children from average, healthy homes can be harmed by spending long hours in childcare”.

“It turns out that normal Americans care more about their families than their jobs, and want a family policy that doesn’t shunt their kids into crap daycare so they can enjoy more ‘freedom’ in the paid labor force,” he wrote on social media at the time.

A person holds a sign during a news conference on childcare relief on Capitol Hill. Photograph: Mariam Zuhaib/AP

As vice-president, Vance has continued to angle himself as a defender of families. “To the radicals who wish to abolish my family and the thousands and millions of other families across our country, we have a simple message for them: stay the hell away from our kids!” Vance said at the Republicans’ midterm convention in September.

But experts and parents who spoke to the Guardian said the proposed program for stay-at-home parents prioritized a single type of family structure and ignored the flexibility that families need to support themselves and their children.

“Families of all compositions need help right now. All families do. We should not pit families against each other,” said Chandra Childers, a senior policy and economic analyst at the Economic Policy Institute.

For Natalia Goodnow of Pittsburgh, being a stay-at-home mother was a temporary choice that allowed her family to save on the cost of childcare while she went back to school and pursued a second master’s degree. After she graduated, she was forced to delay her next job search after graduating because the waitlist for her child’s daycare, like many in the country, had grown significantly after the pandemic.

Now, Goodnow is balancing taking care of her child while also building up her own consulting business, which she believes will afford her flexibility to work around her daughter’s schedule. She doesn’t expect she’ll ever stop working in some capacity.

There’s also a risk to relying on one parent’s income. Even though her family is able to live “somewhat stable” on just her husband’s income, Goodnow said she was worried that his career as a physician, combined with the responsibility to support the whole family himself, will lead to burnout.

“I have to have a second income stream created to give him flexibility to scale down, because it’s just not sustainable,” she said, pointing out that the proposed $9,000 wouldn’t be enough to match what she would make if she worked.

Jillian Whitlow, a mom of three who is pregnant with her fourth child, wishes at times that she could stay at home to take care of her children, but after launching a successful marketing business, her husband left his job as a mechanic to take care of their kids.

The choice stemmed from practicality. The cost of daycare for her children was greater than their monthly mortgage payment.

Though Whitlow’s family, who live in Texas, is in a better financial place than they have ever been, they still feel the pain of rising costs and are still paying off student debt and loans on their mobile home. Still, Whitlow sees the White House’s proposal as an unnecessary interference in personal family decisions.

A baby plays with toys at a daycare center in Monahans, Texas, on 21 August 2018. Photograph: Callaghan O’Hare/Bloomberg via Getty Images

“I would love it if there was a lot less government in all of our lives,” she said, “I would love it if each individual family had more freedom to make the decisions that they want to make about the best way to raise their families.

“It should be up to me and my family to make that decision, and not the government pushing me to stay at home,” Goodnow said.

Childcare costs can place a major financial burden on families. Forty per cent of households, including those that have one parent who works less than full-time, spend an outsized percentage of their income on childcare, according to a recent report from the Georgetown Law Center on Poverty and Inequality.

The White House’s plan, by reallocating federal funds from the Department of Health and Human Services that have typically gone to low-income, single-parent families, could actually making childcare even more expensive or less available, Childers said.

“The minute you start draining resources that go into funding this childcare pool, then rates have to rise on other families, some of whom may already be stretching to make it as it is,” she said.

Recent policy changes by the Trump administration may also indirectly make it harder on American families, said Liz Hipple, a policy expert at the Georgetown Law Center on Poverty and Inequality. Recent pushes to increase the work requirements for Medicaid and Snap benefits, for example, will probably make it harder for some families to support themselves.

Policies that support higher wages or paid parental leave would go much further to support families, she added.

“If the Trump administration was serious about supporting traditional families with one breadwinner, there are plenty of labor policies that they could support,” Hipple said. “Those aren’t the policies that they’re pursuing.”
