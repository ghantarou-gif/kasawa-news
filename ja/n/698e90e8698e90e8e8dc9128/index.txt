3c:T281f,Every morning long before dawn, while America sleeps, legions of Texas construction workers grab their fluorescent vests and hard hats, sling ladders and tools into their trucks, and head down the highway for another day of grueling labour under the blistering sun.

Elsewhere, before the first flush of daylight settles over Texas’s concrete sprawl, a separate army gets on the move in a fleet of shiny unmarked SUVs. They hover at gas stations, prowl around bodegas, pounce on the highways.

They’re on their way to work too. But the federal agents who form this second army are on a different mission.

Unlike the builders, they are not forging the US’s future one new starter home at a time. Their job is to pursue those who do the building: they follow the framers, track down the roofers, chase the cement masons and concrete finishers.

“In Texas, you go deer hunting in the early morning,” said Jacob Monty, an immigration lawyer in Houston. “That’s what they are doing. They are hunting for construction workers, early in the morning, like you would an animal.”

Texas and its construction workforce are the ground zero of Donald Trump’s latest mass deportation effort, dubbed ICE 2.0 . As the US president has pivoted from last year’s militarized “invasions” of Los Angeles and Minneapolis to this year’s less visible but no less devastating stop-and-search tactics, he has found rich pickings in Texas.

Texas is seeing by far the greatest number of ICE arrests in the country. More than 12,000 in July alone , almost double the number in the next most affected states, Florida and California.

Workers constructing townhouses at a subdivision in Harlingen, Texas in September.

Many of the detainees are Hispanic builders. Of every 10 construction workers in Texas, four are foreign born , most of whom are Hispanic and undocumented.

“This is low-hanging fruit,” Monty said. “If you target Texas Hispanics in trucks, you have a good chance of nabbing an undocumented person, and feeding the mass deportation machine.”

But there is a price to pay for ICE casting its dragnet through Texas barrios and building sites as it seeks to meet its new target of 3,000 immigration arrests daily. The street-level apprehensions are roiling the state, distorting its economy, forcing up house prices and the cost of groceries.

Hispanic families are also being torn apart. Hardworking people are being pushed into the shadows, others disappearing as they flee the country or are deported. The foundations of new homes are lying unfinished, building sites empty.

With the midterm elections just days away, and hugely consequential congressional races as well as the gubernatorial contest hanging in the balance, there are also political costs. Trump appears to be digging a hole for himself with Texas’s Hispanic voters.

Latino Texans who were born in the US or have naturalised, and thus can vote, are increasingly angry and confused. Many of them voted for Trump two years ago, attracted by his promise of a stronger economy and a fortified border that would keep out “criminal” immigrants.

They delivered Trump a historic victory, even in majority-Hispanic districts that had been Democratic strongholds for more than a century – voters like Sergio Benavides, 44, the owner of a home-building construction company in the Texas borderlands of the Rio Grande valley.

A naturalised US citizen, he voted for Trump in 2024. He liked his campaign promises that he would lower prices and secure the border. When people warned him it would turn sour, he didn’t listen.

He’s listening now. The prices of homes, food and gas have all gone up. Workers have to be paid more because so many have been deported. One of his subcontractors and his wife have disappeared, picked up by ICE, leaving behind three US-citizen children.

“That makes me sad,” he said. “We are here to protect our families, send our kids to college, to learn and grow. This is not what I thought when I voted for Trump.”

Benavides is part of a backlash that is showing up in the polls. Latino voters are turning against the president on the very issues that drew them to him – the cost of living, and immigration enforcement.

A poll last month from Equis Research found that disapproval of Trump had risen to 63% among Texas Latinos. In the critical US Senate race, which could determine which party holds the upper chamber during Trump’s final two years in office, 58% of likely Latino voters intend to back the Democratic candidate James Talarico, while only 33% support the scandal-ridden Republican, Ken Paxton. This, in a state that last returned a Democrat to the US Senate in 1988.

Latinos make up a third of Texas’s registered voters, the second-highest number in the country. And they know it.

Construction workers among them.

Marco Antonio Osorio’s day begins at 5am. Same routine, six days a week.

A member of his crew comes to pick him up at his house in Houston. They drive around in their shared white truck gathering other crew, then head off to the site of a new apartment complex where they spend the next 10 hours laying TV and wifi fiber optic cables.

Marco Antonio Osorio, 50, and his wife Cecilia Rodríguez, 45, pose for a portrait at their home in Houston, Texas, on 14 September 2026. Photograph: Danielle Villasana/The Guardian

It’s their speciality. They are good at it, and in demand. If only they were left to get on with it.

Around the beginning of the year Osorio, 50, noticed a surge in ICE street activity. “There were so many more sheriff’s vehicles, state troopers, ICE on the highways weaving between cars checking plates.”

As an undocumented Mexican who came to the US in 1993, aged 17, he knew he was vulnerable. But he thought he had some cover. His US-born daughter Gabriela is serving as a marine at Camp Pendleton in California. She has helped him apply for residency, and the paperwork is being processed.

Still, he wasn’t taking chances. He changed his habits. No going out of the house unless for essentials. No nights out at the movies. He couldn’t stop working, or else how would he pay the bills? But now he wears a button-down shirt on the drive to the site rather than his fluorescent yellow vest. No point advertising himself as a construction worker with ICE on the hunt.

He told his company he didn’t want to work outside Houston any more. The freeways to Austin and San Antonio were crawling with cop cars. “The company told me there weren’t any jobs here – either go out of town, or you don’t work.”

Framed pictures of Osorio’s daughter, who serves in the US military, photographed at their home in Houston. Photograph: Danielle Villasana/The Guardian

So he took a job in Austin. On 10 June, as he was returning home after another 10-hour shift, a sheriff’s car did a U-turn on the freeway and pulled him over. The officer said it was because his truck had a faulty rear light, but Osorio asked himself how they could have known that as they were traveling in opposite directions. More likely, he thought, was that his vehicle has a clear front screen. “They could see we were Hispanic.”

Within two minutes of the traffic stop, ICE SUVs blocked him in, front and back. The speed with which they appeared, Osorio realised, meant it had to be coordinated.

He told them his daughter was a US marine. He showed the officers the receipt for his residency application. An ICE officer replied: “Oh, that doesn’t count.” They took him to a freezing cell, the notorious las hieleras , and from there to an immigration detention facility near the border. He spent 40 days in the wilderness of ICE custody, until Monty got him released for lack of due process.

As he awaits his appeal, Osorio continues to work. Those bills keep coming, his children need feeding. On drives to work sites in Houston, he thinks about his daughter serving the country whose government wants him gone. He worries about his youngest child, an autistic boy, and what will become of him. He imagines returning to Mexico, a place he hasn’t known for 33 years.

As he drives, he spots apartment blocks all over the city that he helped put up. And he’s proud. “See those – I worked there 10 years ago. Or those – we built them 15 years ago.”

He thinks to himself: “This is my home.”

Osorio and his wife say goodnight to their son and give him a blessing. Photograph: Danielle Villasana

Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, 52, a Mexican national, had been in the US for 35 years, two longer than Osorio. A house framer, the skilled worker who puts up the wooden skeleton of new houses, he carried out his days with an identical drill. Out the door at 5am, pick up the crew, 10 hours of hard graft, then relax on the balcony of his self-built Houston home listening to tierra caliente music with Ñoño, his beloved dog whom he named after a podgy 1970s Mexican sitcom character.

When ICE began to crank up its street arrests in February, Salgado spotted an opportunity. Many workers were too scared to leave the house, and new homes were standing half finished. A fearless entrepreneur, Salgado began accepting jobs that other undocumented framers like him would not contemplate.

He took precautions. He swapped his construction vest for a suit jacket while driving to work. He removed the jumble of blueprints, invoices and receipts from the dashboard of his white van. It was like his mobile office on wheels, but it branded him as a building professional, so he cleaned it off.

As time passed, and the ICE presence grew more oppressive, his three sons grew concerned. They are all American-born US citizens, and Salgado had high aspirations for them. They weren’t going to be builders like him – they were going to be educated, fulfill their own American dream. Ronaldo Salgado, the eldest, is a history teacher; Lorenzo Salgado Jr studied at the prestigious Tufts University and is now an engineer in Washington DC; and the youngest is at college, also studying engineering.
